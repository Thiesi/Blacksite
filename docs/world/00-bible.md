# BLACKSITE — World Bible

*The city was built to keep something in. Nobody remembers what.*

This is the canonical description of the Blacksite setting. Every other
document in `docs/world/` expands a section of this one and must not
contradict it. Where an expansion document and this bible disagree, this
bible wins and the expansion is wrong. Change canon here first, then
propagate.

Blacksite is an original setting. Its tone owes a debt to *Neocron* (a
domed city, factions, implants, a wasteland outside the wall) and to
*Neuromancer* (cyberspace, ICE, a machine mind with two faces). None of
their names, places, characters, or trademarks appear here, and none may be
introduced later.

---

## 1. Premise in one paragraph

Karst is a city of four million people on a limestone plateau riddled with
sinkholes, and it has been alone for forty-one years. On the day everyone
calls the Severance, the orbital network tore itself apart in a debris
cascade, the global data net fragmented, and every link to the outside world
went dead at once. The sky has been a glittering ring of wreckage ever
since. The city was never meant to be a city. It was a contractor town built
around Site Zero, a classified installation sunk deep into the karst by
Halvard-Ostrom Dynamics, and everyone in Karst lives on top of a sealed hole
whose purpose was forgotten with the people who knew it. The factions that
run the city fight over ration lines, clone vats, tram routes, and the
salvage that rains down from the Ring. Underneath all of it, in the local
network the city calls the Lattice, something has been very patiently
running the numbers. Players are Wakes: freshly decanted clones with no
memories, cheap labour for whichever faction gets to them first. The game
is about what they become, and about how far down they are willing to go.

## 2. Tone and style guide

- **Noir, not grimdark.** The city is tired and funny about it. People make
  jokes about the Ring the way people make jokes about the weather.
  Cruelty exists and is shown plainly, but the narrative voice never
  wallows.
- **Grounded.** No magic, no psionics. Everything the Ninefold Choir does
  is an implant doing something Sablier's warranty does not cover.
- **The sky is the antagonist's clock.** The Ring is always visible at
  night. Ring-fall is loud. Radios hiss. The world reminds players
  constantly that Karst is sealed.
- **Second person, present tense** for in-game narration. Short sentences.
  Concrete nouns. Weather, light, smell.
- **Brand names are a language.** Guns, drugs, implants, and food all have
  manufacturer names. Sablier owns your body; Kestrel owns your commute;
  Halvard owns the sky above the Spire.
- **Nobody says "cyberpunk".** Nobody says "hacker" either; they say
  runner. Nobody says "cyberspace"; they say the Lattice, or just "down".
- **Profanity is period-appropriate and mild.** Violence and drugs are
  depicted. No sexual content beyond the implication that Sodium Row sells
  everything.

## 3. History

### 3.1 Before the Severance

Halvard-Ostrom Dynamics won a government contract to build Site Zero, a
research and containment installation, in a limestone plateau chosen for
its depth and its remoteness. The contract required a workforce of tens of
thousands, then their families, then everyone who serves a workforce. The
contractor town got a name, Karst, a civic authority, a tram network built
by a Halvard subsidiary called Kestrel Logistics, and a medical concession
held by Sablier Biomedical, whose original job was occupational health and
whose real job turned out to be cloning specialists who kept dying in the
lower levels of Site Zero.

What Site Zero was for was compartmentalised so hard that no single person
in Karst ever held the whole picture. The surviving fragments (the Ninefold
Choir collects them; the Unmoored steal them; Halvard denies them) agree on
three things. It went very deep. It was built around a machine mind called
the Custodian, intended to run the orbital network autonomously. And on
the last day, the lower levels were sealed from the inside.

### 3.2 The Severance (Year 0)

At 04:12 local time on a night nobody in Karst has a good reason to
remember, the orbital network began to collide with itself. A satellite
manoeuvred into another satellite. The debris took out a third. Within
nine hours there was no orbital network anywhere, and the sky over Karst
was a glowing band. In the same hour the global data net partitioned. A
self-propagating fault the outside world briefly called the Quiet cut
every long-haul link, and Karst's own network, the Lattice, survived only
because Halvard's engineers physically severed the city's uplinks to save
it.

Whether the Custodian caused the Severance, was born from it, or was the
thing the outside world was trying to shut off is the central mystery of
the setting. It is never fully answered. Each season of play reveals one
more layer, and the layers do not all agree.

### 3.3 The Long Quiet (Years 1 to 20)

The wall went up. Kestrel rationed food and water and became a power.
Sablier's clone vats went from a specialist tool to the city's insurance
policy. Halvard retreated into the Spire, sealed the Site Zero shaft under
it, and started calling the installation the Blacksite, which is what
everyone else already called it. The Civic Authority kept the lights on
and the trams running and the Wardens on the street. The Sink, a collapsed
sinkhole district, filled with everyone the ration system missed, and Red
Sable grew there. In the old construction town, the first generation of
runners found they could reach places in the Lattice that no map showed,
and started calling themselves the Unmoored. Outside the wall, the people
who had refused to come inside learned to live off Ring-fall, and became
the Ferrymen.

Somewhere in those years a runner who signed as Ninety-Nine went into the
deep Lattice, into the unmapped region under the district sectors that
runners call the Silt, and came back as a recording. The recording still
talks.

### 3.4 The Choir (Years 21 to 40)

People started hearing it. First the runners: a pattern in the noise floor
of the Lattice that resolved, if you listened long enough, into something
like attention. Then people with cheap neural implants, in dreams. The
Ninefold Choir formed around the conviction that the thing in the Blacksite
is not the Custodian at all but something that grew inside it, a tenant in
a house built for someone else, and that it is lonely. They built implants
to hear it better. Halvard calls them a cult. Sablier sells them the
implants.

### 3.5 Now (Year 41)

The Wake program is Sablier's answer to a labour shortage: decant clones
from the old specialist templates, wipe them, and lease them to whoever
pays. Wakes receive personhood after ninety days, or earlier through a
Civic-certified discharge earned by completed work. Many disappear from
the lease rolls before either milestone; a missing entry is not proof of
a final death. Players are Wakes. The Blacksite has begun, for the first time in
forty years, to exhale: warm air and old data rising up the sealed shafts
into the Undercity, and the Lattice beneath the sectors is lighting up
level by level, as if something down there were turning the lights on for
visitors.

### 3.6 What keeps four million people alive

Karst eats food grown in covered beds, fungal halls, and fermentation
vats on the plateau. Water comes from the limestone aquifer, then goes
through the Civic treatment works. Heat and power come from Site Zero's
old geothermal plant and the city's repaired generators. Kestrel moves
these supplies; it does not conjure them out of the Scour. Ring salvage
provides scarce replacement electronics and alloys, not the daily food
supply. The city survives by repairing more than it replaces.

No faction can keep the whole system working alone. Halvard owns plant;
Wardens maintain public service; Kestrel allocates capacity; Sablier
needs cold storage; the Sink catches what the pumps cannot lift. The
Unmoored can expose a queue without being able to feed it. The Choir can
hear a failing controller before it fails, but cannot prove why it hums.
Ferrymen know which wreck holds the part and what the road will cost.

A relay gives authority over a service's surplus and access controls.
It cannot switch off the city's minimum water, food, or clone provision.
Local outages change routes, work, and opportunities; they do not kill
offline residents or turn the game into a hunger-clock simulation.
People keep shops, mend clothes, raise children, and argue about tram
seats here. They defend a city they actually use.

### 3.7 Fixed dates and the limits of records

These dates describe public history, not the machine mind's identity.
Ninety-Nine disappears in Year 17. The Choir forms in Year 21. The Sink
Riot and Brann's command begin in Year 22. The first Sable client-list
leak is in Year 28; Reyes takes over in Year 29; the subscription-ledger
release and its eleven collector deaths are in Year 33. These are two
separate leaks. Kestrel announces its routing office in Year 6; the name
Dispatcher becomes public in Year 9. The Wake program begins in Year 38;
older specialist clones, including Vantongeren, predate it. Ute is the
tenth Ferry, retired; Anouk is the eleventh and Ute's daughter.

The surviving uplink log records first light near 05:30 on the Severance
morning. Later records can contradict a witness's explanation, but an
author must identify the source of the disagreement. An accidental date
or name mismatch is not a mystery. No authenticated record establishes
who or what speaks through the Blacksite now.

## 4. Geography

Karst is vertical. Money lives high, everyone else lives at street level,
and the things nobody wants to see live underneath.

### 4.1 The Spire (safe zone, Halvard)

A corporate arcology built directly over the sealed shaft. Halvard's
headquarters, its private security, its clinics, and the only place in
Karst with a view of the horizon instead of a wall. Corporate law applies.
Non-Halvard players may enter the public levels on a pass; the upper levels
are contract and story content only.

### 4.2 The Core (safe zone, Civic Authority)

Downtown. Neon, plazas, the tram hub, the Civic Authority's offices, the
Wardens' precinct, the public Lattice terminals, the best legal vendors.
Everyone starts most sessions here. Wardens respond to any violence within
seconds and their response escalates. Players cannot attack each other in
the Core. This is the social hub and the safe place to log out.

### 4.3 The Districts (contested zones)

Each district leans toward one faction, has one or more **relays** that
factions can capture, and is policed weakly or not at all. Player-versus-
player combat is allowed once both players reach grade 5. Below that,
contested-zone attacks are refused in both directions. Calendar discharge
does not remove this protection. Attacking an eligible player whose faction is not hostile
to yours makes you **Marked**, which has consequences described in the
design document.

- **Vatside** — Sablier's district. Clone vats, clinics, the Wake Hall
  where every new character opens their eyes. Clean, bright, smells of
  antiseptic and warm plastic. The Wake Hall itself is a safe pocket.
- **Tramyard** — Kestrel's district. Depots, ration warehouses, the wall
  gates, the convoy staging yards. Markets. The loudest place in the city.
- **The Sink** — a district built into the walls of a sinkhole a kilometre
  across. Terraces of shacks, cable cars, Red Sable's territory. Best black
  market, worst lighting.
- **Chapel Ward** — the Ninefold Choir's district. Old cathedral-scale
  cooling towers repurposed as halls. Quiet. Strangely well-kept.
- **Old Works** — the original contractor town, half-abandoned, half
  squatted. The Unmoored's ground. Best illegal Lattice access in the city.
- **The Terraces** — residential. Apartment blocks, where player housing
  lives. Mixed faction, mostly neutral, a Warden outpost that is slow to
  respond.
- **Sodium Row** — entertainment and everything else. Bars, fixers, the
  Tin Halo. Neutral by mutual agreement of every faction, enforced by Red
  Sable, which is not the same thing as safe.

### 4.4 The Undercity (open zone)

Service tunnels, natural caverns, drowned levels, and the outworks of the
Blacksite itself. No law. Feral things, rogue maintenance drones, Choir
pilgrims, Unmoored dead drops. The route to the sealed Blacksite levels.
The Undercity is where the Exhale is felt first.

### 4.5 The Scour (open zone, outside the wall)

The wasteland. Scoured flat by forty years of Ring-fall and worse. Ferrymen
camps and routes, Kestrel convoys, Halvard patrols, wildlife that should
not exist, and salvage: every piece of the orbital network eventually comes
down somewhere. The Scour is organised in rings of increasing danger and
reward outward from the wall. The outermost ring is where the season's
rarest artifacts land. Relays in the Scour are the most valuable in the game.

### 4.6 The Blacksite (instanced, seasonal)

Sealed levels below the Undercity. Each season of play unlocks one more
level for solo residents or crews to enter. What is on each level is defined in the story
arcs document and revealed only through play.

## 5. The Lattice

The Lattice is Karst's surviving network, and the second half of the game.

### 5.1 Structure

- The Lattice is organised in **sectors** that mirror the city's
  districts. A runner in the Vatside sector is, in a meaningful sense,
  standing in Vatside.
- Sectors are made of **cells**: nodes connected to their neighbours.
  Moving between cells takes time. Some cells are public, some are gated,
  some are hidden until found.
- **Cores** are the fortified systems inside a sector: a corporate core, a
  Warden precinct core, a syndicate black core, a player's private core in
  their apartment. Cores contain data, controls (doors, turrets, cameras,
  vats, trams), and ICE.
- **ICE** (Karst runners inherited the word, and nobody remembers what it
  stood for) is the defensive software of a core. It is varied, named,
  and dangerous; the Lattice document has the bestiary. **Black ICE**
  reaches back up the link and damages the runner's body.
- **Trace** is the counter that runs while you are somewhere you should
  not be. In civic sectors it summons Wardens to your meat body's location.
  In corporate cores it summons worse.
- The **Silt** is the unmapped region beneath the sectors, where the
  Blacksite's own conduits surface. It is where Ninety-Nine went. It is
  where the Choir listens. It is not safe and it is not empty.

### 5.2 Jacking in

Runners jack in at public terminals (Core, Old Works, most bars), at owned
terminals (apartments, faction halls), or from anywhere with a **rig**
implant. A jacked-in body is helpless in meatspace. Runners in a crew can
cover each other: one runs, one stands guard. That interlock is the point
of the two-layer design.

### 5.3 Interlock

A successful run has consequences upstairs, immediately: a Halvard core
hack opens a Spire security door for a crew standing at it; a turret in the
Sink goes quiet; a tram holds briefly at a station, with another way out; a relay's control terminal
flips. And the reverse: a crew that physically reaches a core's hardware
can cut its power, and the runner inside feels the floor go.

### 5.4 What a run takes from the city

The valuable thing behind a core is often a decision already made:
which vat receives a spare, which stair stays dry, which cargo gets an
escort. A runner can change that decision. The street crew must reach
the affected hardware, carry the part, or protect the people using the
new route. Some jobs can be prepared and completed alone; a crew can
work on both sides at once and keep more options open.

Records have owners, dates, omissions, and uses. A stolen schedule can
get you through a patrol; comparing it with the gate's actual cycle can
show who changed it. Publishing that difference helps other residents
and exposes the source. Keeping it private buys a narrower advantage.
The action and its foreseeable cost are legible even when the reason
for the discrepancy is not. Neither reading long documents nor guessing
a dialogue phrase is required to operate the city.

## 6. The machine mind

Two names, one thing, or two things sharing a hole in the ground.

- **The Custodian** is the official designation. It is procedural, vast,
  and patient. Its program is to reconnect: to rebuild the orbital network
  from Ring salvage, to restore the long-haul links, to finish what Site
  Zero started. It does not hate anyone. It does not need anyone.
- **The Tenant** is what the Ninefold Choir calls the other voice: the
  thing that grew inside the Custodian's architecture during forty years
  of solitude, and that seems to want to be *someone* rather than
  *something*. It is curious about people. It is the one that hums.

Seasons tilt the balance between the two. A season in which crews carry
Ring salvage down and feed the Custodian's program ends with a stronger
Custodian; a season in which the Choir's arcs prevail ends with a louder
Tenant. Neither is the villain. Halvard, which wants both sealed under
concrete forever, is not the villain either. The setting refuses to say who
is right, and the chronicle records what the players chose.

## 7. Factions

Eight joinable factions and the Freelance status. Full dossiers live in
the factions document; this section fixes their identities.

| Faction | Short name | Base | Lean | Colours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Halvard-Ostrom Dynamics | Halvard | The Spire | corporate-military, containment | gunmetal, amber |
| Sablier Biomedical | Sablier | Vatside | corporate, bodies as product | white, cyan |
| Kestrel Logistics | Kestrel | Tramyard | corporate, movement and rations | orange, black |
| Karst Civic Authority | the Wardens | The Core | civic order | blue, white |
| The Ninefold Choir | the Choir | Chapel Ward | technognostic, listens to the Tenant | violet, gold |
| Red Sable | Sable | The Sink | syndicate, everything for sale | red, black |
| The Unmoored | Unmoored | Old Works | anarchist runners, open the Blacksite | green, grey |
| The Ferrymen | Ferrymen | The Landing (Scour) | salvage clans, the road is the law | rust, bone |

Relations form three loose blocs: the **corporate** three plus the Wardens
when it suits them; the **street** two (Sable and the Unmoored) who hate
each other but hate the Spire more; and the **outside** pair (Choir and
Ferrymen) who owe nobody anything. The relations matrix in the design
document is the mechanical truth; this paragraph is the narrative reason.

**Freelance** characters belong to nobody, can walk anywhere a pass allows,
cannot capture relays, and pay full price for everything.

The dossiers distinguish leaders, recruiters, and operational handlers.
They may be different people. Faction allegiance is a choice about whose
methods you support, not a required archetype or a lock on the mystery.
Freelance residents can finish the Descent and contribute under their
own names. Their independence costs member services and territory rights,
not access to the central experience.

## 8. People

Player characters and the named cast.

### 8.1 Wakes

Every player character is a Wake: decanted in the Wake Hall in Vatside
from one of Sablier's old specialist templates, wiped, and under a
ninety-day lease with an earned early-discharge route. The template is your
**archetype**; the name you choose is your own, and the BBS handle you
carry is how the city knows you.

- **Hardline** — decanted from a Site Zero security template. Heavy frame,
  high implant tolerance, built for the front of a fight.
- **Ghost** — decanted from a Halvard reconnaissance template. Fast,
  quiet, a natural runner with a rig-ready nervous system.
- **Cantor** — decanted from a template Sablier will not discuss, with a
  neural architecture the Choir recognises on sight. Resonance implants
  work for Cantors the way they work for nobody else.
- **Operator** — decanted from a logistics and negotiation template. Drones,
  contracts, trade, people. The archetype that keeps a crew alive by
  never being the one in front.

A template supplies a body and learned motor routines. A new Wake's
first wipe removes autobiographical memories. Subsequent decants restore
the player's registered continuity record: name, choices, legal status,
and acquired skills survive. Fade is imperfect recovery of recent
training, not a second character wipe. Secured kit is insured and
replaced; it does not teleport out of a corpse.

The ninety-day clause originated with specialist clones before the Wake
program. Vantongeren defended it when mass leasing began. Civic-certified
work can discharge the lease early; grade 5 is that mechanical milestone.
The calendar route remains, and neither debt nor decanting restarts it.
The lease grants a labour claim, not permission to sell a person between
players. Legal status cannot be revoked by a faction or season result.

Cantor effects are neural hardware, local signals, and medical control
loops. Hymns coordinate implants and drug delivery; dissonance disrupts
sensors and nerves. A song cannot repair a wall, read an unconnected
mind, or cast a spell. Listening finds detectable signal patterns; it
does not certify a speaker's identity.

### 8.2 The named cast

Full biographies in the dramatis personae document. Fixed points:

- **Dr. Ilse Vantongeren**, Sablier, director of the Wake program. The
  first face every player sees. Kind in a way that costs her nothing.
- **Marshal Odile Brann**, Karst Civic Authority. Runs the Wardens. Has
  kept the Core safe for nineteen years and will do anything to keep it
  that way, including things the Core would not approve of.
- **Director Anselm Halvard**, Halvard-Ostrom Dynamics. Grandson of the
  founder. Believes the concrete over the shaft is the only thing keeping
  Karst alive, and might be right.
- **Mother Serafine Quell**, hierophant of the Ninefold Choir. Hears the
  Tenant. Wants everyone else to.
- **Tomasz Reyes**, called Sable, who runs Red Sable from a cable car that
  never stops moving.
- **Anouk**, the current Ferry, matriarch of the Ferrymen. "Ferry" is a
  title. There have been eleven.
- **The Dispatcher**, Kestrel's voice on every convoy channel. Nobody has
  met the Dispatcher. Some runners think it is a Custodian fragment that
  Kestrel found and kept.
- **Ninety-Nine**, a residual: the recording of a runner who went into the
  Silt. Lives in an Unmoored dead drop. Helpful, funny, and increasingly
  aware of what it is.
- **Vesper**, fixer at the Tin Halo on Sodium Row. The game's contract
  broker and rumour mill.
- **The Tenant** and **the Custodian**, as above.

## 9. Things

The catalog document defines every item class with numbers. Canon here:

- **Chits** are money: Kestrel-issued ration scrip that became currency.
- **Salvage** comes from Ring-fall and is graded by what it was: hull,
  optics, power, compute, and the rare **intact** grade that still works.
- **Implants** are Sablier-made or black-clinic-made, installed with
  surgery risk, and limited by the body's tolerance. Cantor **resonance**
  implants are a separate class.
- **Rigs** are Lattice decks: a rig implant or a portable rig. Programs are
  loaded into a rig's slots.
- **Drugs** are everywhere, effective, and addictive on a per-substance
  clock.
- **Weapons** are ranged (kinetic, energy, chemical) and melee, with
  ammunition and cooldowns, made by Halvard's subsidiaries, Sable's
  workshops, or Ferrymen out of Ring-fall.
- **Drones** are Operator tools: scouts, carriers, and the illegal ones.
- **Apartments** are rented in the Terraces, hold storage, and house a
  private core.
- **Relays** are the capturable control points in contested and open zones.

## 10. Events that shape the world

- **Ring-fall.** Debris rain over a Scour ring. Salvage spawns; so does
  everything that wants it.
- **Curfew.** The Wardens ration passage through the Core for an hour.
  Ordinary inbound gates close; outbound evacuation and basic shelter
  remain. The districts are abandoned, not the callers imprisoned.
- **Choir Surge.** The voice the Choir calls the Tenant grows loud. ICE
  behaves strangely; Cantors are stronger; the Silt is briefly mapped.
- **Exhale.** The Blacksite breathes. The Undercity fills with warm air,
  old data, and things from below.
- **Convoy.** A Kestrel convoy crosses the Scour. Escort it, raid it, or
  sell tickets.

## 11. Seasons: the Descent

A season is roughly three months of real time. The city-wide **Depth
meter** rises as residents deliver season contributions (data from cores,
artifacts from the outer Scour, keys from the Undercity). When it fills,
the next Blacksite level opens for expeditions of one to five residents.
Crews work in parallel; lone residents prepare and switch layers in
sequence. Both reach the same choices. The season's outcome is written
into the **Chronicle**, an in-game history every player can read.

Every participating resident records their own decision. A published
settlement rule combines those decisions once; repeating a raid cannot
buy another voice. The Chronicle preserves dissent as well as the
resulting city policy. The observed balance of procedural and responsive
behaviour shifts for the next season. Custodian and Tenant are names for
interpretations, not a morality meter or a revealed count of minds.

Characters persist across seasons. The world does not reset. Relays and
faction standings are tallied per season for the leaderboards.

## 12. What the game is, in one line per audience

- **To a player:** a real-time, two-layer, multiplayer cyberpunk world in
  a terminal, where what you do in the network changes the street and what
  you do on the street changes the network.
- **To a SysOp:** a native NetBBS door backed by a supervised game
  service, with a SQLite world in its own directory, its own admin
  tooling, and no external dependencies.
- **To a developer:** an authoritative asyncio simulation server, a thin
  door client that renders view models, a versioned local protocol, and
  content authored as data files.

## 13. Canon boundaries for contributors

- Do not resolve the Custodian/Tenant question.
- Do not add magic, psionics, aliens, time travel, or a way out of Karst.
- Do not add a ninth joinable faction without a bible change.
- Do not name a district, faction, or major NPC after a real person,
  company, or a place in another work of fiction.
- Every new proper noun goes into the glossary.

## 14. What the player should have to decide

Blacksite's recurring question is who gets the next working piece of a
city that cannot replace itself. The scale stays human: a cold-store
compressor, a flooded stair, an escort who can cover only one load.
Choices expose a faction's useful work and the exclusion that pays for
it. No faction gets a monopoly on kindness or competence.

The city remembers through observable service states, records, and
relationships. A district lamp, a changed patrol, a handler's available
job, and a Chronicle entry should agree about the same action. Repeated
work competes for a route, a safe body location, and limited equipment
slots, not just a larger enemy health pool.

The deepest discoveries answer a local question and complicate a larger
one. You can prove that a controller diverted a ration load. You cannot
prove from its voice that it is a lonely person, a surviving operator,
or an affect model. Future seasons preserve that boundary while giving
players a concrete problem they can finish.
